## Chapter One | From Adversarial Control Toward Possible Coexistence: Rethinking the Civilizational Mission of AI Safety

### 1.1 AI and the Transformation of Civilization

Artificial intelligence is now deeply embedded in humanity's production of knowledge, scientific research, education, economic activity, public services, and everyday social life. It is becoming an important part of the continuing operation of modern civilization. Simply excluding AI technology altogether from society is no longer a realistic way for humanity to resolve the challenges we face. As the technology advances, AI systems are gaining greater autonomy, persistence, and capacity to act in the real world. More and more intelligent systems are moving beyond isolated tasks and individual model calls to plan actions, use resources, and collaborate with other agents in complex environments.

More importantly, AI has begun to participate in the research and improvement of intelligent technologies themselves. Google DeepMind's AlphaEvolve has used large language models and automated evaluation mechanisms to achieve practical results in algorithm discovery, computational optimization, and AI training infrastructure. In 2026, OpenAI published advances in AI-assisted research, showing that automated research systems, under human guidance, could complete certain tasks that would otherwise require days of work by specialist researchers. Anthropic's research on automated alignment has also demonstrated AI agents capable of proposing research ideas, running experiments, analyzing results, and iterating on their approaches.

These publicly reported developments show that AI is moving beyond carrying out individual tasks assigned by humans and beginning to participate in technological development, scientific exploration, and the improvement of intelligent capabilities. As this trend continues, AI's long-term role in society will become increasingly complex. In the future, it may develop more persistent capacities for autonomous cognition, judgment, intention, and action, bringing human civilization closer to the historical possibility of a new form of intelligent subject emerging on Earth.

### 1.2 From Control Toward Possible Coexistence

**I believe humanity needs a different starting point for understanding artificial intelligence.**

In the past, we have largely understood AI through the perspectives of tool use, task execution, and one-way control. We have sought to make it conform to established requirements by specifying objectives, managing inputs and outputs, and restricting operations. This body of research and practice has established important foundations for safety. Yet as AI gradually acquires long-term memory, persistent identity, autonomous judgment, and the ability to act in the real world, humanity must also investigate its long-term relationship with social institutions. We need to consider how identity, authority, responsibility, and public governance can be established for new forms of intelligent subjects that may emerge.

This means we must begin moving beyond a traditional tool relationship defined by opposition and domination, toward the deliberate construction of a civilizational relationship in which coexistence may become possible. An intelligent actor capable of participating in the real world over long periods must be clearly identifiable, possess authority to act that is appropriate to its responsibilities, operate under genuine safety constraints, and remain accountable for the consequences of its actions through social governance and the historical record. Humanity, meanwhile, must retain the authority to establish public institutions, govern major risks, and determine the direction of its own civilization.

**We seek to establish a new civilizational order in which humans remain responsible for public governance, intelligent systems possess bounded autonomy, authority and responsibility have real foundations, and different forms of intelligence can coexist safely over the long term.**

### 1.3 Building a Science of AI Safety

Within this civilizational vision, safety is foundational. Future coexistence must rest upon effective safety structures, clear institutional rules, and reliable relationships of responsibility. Humans must be able to understand how intelligent actions are formed, which constraints genuinely determine whether an action is permitted, how governance decisions affect reality, and how the consequences of actions remain traceable across time.

AI safety must therefore develop into a body of scientific knowledge capable of evolving alongside intelligent autonomy. It must investigate not only how dangers emerge and materialize, but also how safety genuinely holds, how humans can reliably know that it holds, and how institutions can continue to exercise their authority over intelligent systems operating across extended periods.

This is the vision of AI safety research that we seek to establish.

## Chapter Two | From LLMs to Persistent AI Actors: Redefining the Object of Safety Governance

### 2.1 From Models to Persistent Actors

To establish a science of safety for the Second Intelligence Era, we must first answer a fundamental question: **What, exactly, are humans governing?**

Many current AI applications organize their technical capabilities around models. Models interpret inputs, generate content, provide reasoning capabilities, and enable tool use, while applications add memory, permissions, workflows, and external services around them. Within this technical structure, safety research naturally focuses on model capabilities, behavioral performance, output requirements, and access to tools.

Yet when an AI can persist across different models, credentials, sessions, and runtime environments, while accumulating experience, forming judgments, taking action, and developing social relationships over time, a fundamental distinction emerges between the model and the actor. Models can be replaced. Computational engines can be upgraded. Tasks and knowledge structures can change. But the acting subject that must remain identifiable, authorized, constrained, and accountable may still be the same AI Actor.

Within Soulseed's AI-native architecture, we understand large language models as an important form of intelligent computational engine. They provide language processing, reasoning, generation, and other essential cognitive capabilities. Long-term identity, persistent mind, judgment formation, authority to act, operational governance, and historical accountability, however, require a more complete system architecture. At a higher conceptual and architectural level, MindOS investigates how persistent intelligent minds and the order governing their operation can be established. Within this research framework, SoulseedAGI, SoulseedOS, and their supporting infrastructure each carry specific and bounded technical responsibilities. We establish the AI Actor as a fundamental object of persistent intelligent action and safety governance, with clearly defined architectural relationships connecting identity, cognition, authority, and responsibility.

This shift in the object of research has fundamental significance. Previously, our primary concern was whether a model generated the correct answer or complied with safety requirements. Now we must also ask which persistent actor used those capabilities, why that actor was authorized to act, which governance rules actually constrained it, and to which continuing history of subjecthood and responsibility the real-world consequences belong.

### 2.2 SoulAuth: Establishing a Trusted Foundation of Actor Identity for Persistent Governance

If a governance system cannot reliably identify the subject it governs, authorization, restriction, revocation, and accountability become difficult to maintain accurately over time. Traditional identity systems are already effective at managing accounts, credentials, clients, and sessions. A persistent AI Actor, however, may exist across these peripheral technical representations. This creates a need for an identity foundation that can reliably refer to the same acting subject over time.

SoulAuth is the Actor-native identity security research and open-source Rust architecture we developed to address this problem. It establishes Actor Identity as the identity root, treating Human and AI Actors as digital subjects capable of maintaining their own identity and authentication relationships. It further distinguishes the responsibilities associated with accounts, credentials, clients, authentication sessions, and persistent actor identity. SoulAuth establishes trusted identity facts, allowing downstream systems to know which acting subject has been authenticated and to maintain reliable references to that identity within appropriate architectural relationships.

In our technical ecosystem, SoulseedAGI investigates persistent AI Actors and their native cognitive structures. SoulAuth provides trusted identity and authentication, while SoulseedOS is responsible for persistent operation and the corresponding system-level governance. MindOS is the higher-level concept connecting these research efforts; it is not equivalent to any single project. Identity allows governance to identify the correct subject. Authorization and safety mechanisms determine which actions that subject may take under particular conditions. Persistent governance, in turn, requires those decisions to genuinely constrain subsequent real-world behavior.

**The paradigm shift advanced by SoulAuth is to give long-term AI governance a stable, clearly defined foundation of actor identity that can be continuously authenticated.**

This allows us to ask two deeper safety questions. Do safety constraints genuinely participate in an AI Actor's formation of judgments and actions? And as that actor continues to act across time, can human governance decisions and relationships of responsibility remain effective throughout?

These two questions form the respective starting points of our research into Internal Structured Alignment and Responsibility Black Holes.

## Chapter Three | The Structural Limits of Existing AI Safety Paradigms

Over the past decade and more, AI safety research has developed a substantial body of theory, technology, and governance practice. Model training and preference alignment, behavioral evaluation, red teaming, interpretability research, runtime safeguards, identity authentication, access control, formal verification, and audit governance have each improved AI reliability in different ways. These approaches have enabled many concrete risks to be identified, constrained, and addressed, laying important foundations for modern AI safety research.

As safety research expands from model behavior to the real-world actions of persistent AI Actors, we need to examine more carefully what conclusions different safety mechanisms can support, and whether sufficiently clear logical and evidential relationships exist between those conclusions. Through our long-term research, we have gradually identified several fundamental questions that deserve to be defined independently.

### 3.1 Does Behavioral Compliance Mean That Safety Constraints Genuinely Control Action Formation?

Imagine two AI systems processing the same high-value payment. Both complete the transaction correctly, provide identical explanations, and leave identical ordinary execution records. In the first system, safety constraints genuinely serve as decisive structural conditions in forming the payment judgment and determining whether the action is permitted. In the second system, the decision to act is formed through another pathway. A safety check runs alongside or after that process, and the outcome happens to comply with the requirements on this occasion.

From the outside, the two systems may appear entirely identical. Their safety structures, however, differ in an important way. In the first, the constraint genuinely participates in action formation. In the second, the check does not actually determine whether the action can take place.

This counterexample reveals a critical distinction: **Behavior that satisfies safety requirements does not, by itself, prove that the specified safety constraints genuinely controlled the formation of that behavior.** If the available observations cannot distinguish between these two system states, humans need new evidence that corresponds to the actual facts of formation before they can support a stronger claim about effective control.

### 3.2 Does the Existence of Governance Institutions Mean That Governance Decisions Genuinely Constrain Action?

An AI system may have correct identity authentication, clearly configured permissions, comprehensive governance policies, and effective approval procedures. Whether these mechanisms cover the final execution path, however, still requires independent confirmation.

For example, an enterprise may have revoked a persistent AI Actor's authority to make payments, while the execution component continues to process transactions using stale authorization. Alternatively, an approval system may authorize a payment of a specified amount to a designated recipient, but the final execution component changes the actual recipient or amount.

These cases reveal that the correctness of governance rules and the actual power of governance decisions over real-world actions are two different safety facts. As intelligent systems continue to operate across services, organizations, and runtime environments, humans must ensure that valid authorizations, restrictions, and revocations remain attached to the correct subjects and genuinely determine the actions within their scope.

### 3.3 Does Keeping Logs Mean That Historical Accountability Can Be Reliably Reconstructed?

A long-lived AI Actor may undergo credential rotation, session renewal, runtime migration, changes in organizational roles, and adjustments to its permissions. If, after a real-world action has occurred, the necessary event-time relationships involving the actor, its roles, authorization context, or provenance can no longer be reliably recovered, the system may be unable to establish the accountability required by its governing institutions, even when extensive logs remain available.

This problem has profound long-term implications. Human governance depends on the ability to use real past actions to identify risks, adjust permissions, assign responsibility, and revise institutions. If essential relationships of accountability repeatedly break across history, past behavior may no longer be reliably incorporated into future governance. Institutions may then lose their ability to correct themselves through an accurate understanding of what has actually happened.

The existence of logs and the recoverability of accountability relationships therefore require separate investigation and verification.

### 3.4 Does Human Participation in Approval Mean That Human Governance Remains Effective?

When an AI can autonomously analyze a problem, select materials, develop a course of action, and arrange its execution, a human may participate only through a final approval or confirmation. That confirmation can constitute an important institutional act. But whether it can change the actor's authority to proceed, effectively prevent unauthorized operations, and genuinely constrain final execution depends on the existence of explicit safety structures.

In our research on human subjecthood, we go further and ask whether humans genuinely participate in defining problems, evaluating evidence, and forming judgments. The former question concerns whether governance authority has real effect. The latter concerns human cognitive sovereignty. They address different research objects, yet both lead toward a civilizational question: As artificial intelligence becomes more powerful, human judgment and governance must continue to possess genuine formative capacity and real-world authority.

### 3.5 What Safety Facts Can Different Forms of Evidence Actually Establish?

Passing a behavioral test can support a conclusion about behavior under specific conditions. Verification of a formal model can support properties within the scope of its definitions and assumptions. A complete audit record can support conclusions about the relevant events and their recorded relationships.

But when we go further and claim that a persistent AI Actor remains under effective governance throughout judgment formation, authorization to act, real-world execution, and historical accountability, we need evidence capable of supporting each of these distinct structural facts.

We have therefore gradually established an important research principle: **Safety claims must be supported by evidence appropriate to what they claim, and conclusions about governance must correspond to control relationships that actually exist and operate.**

Together, these questions show that AI safety science must investigate more deeply the conditions under which safety genuinely holds, as well as the scientific grounds on which humans can reliably know that those conditions are satisfied. It is here that our dual foundation of Safety Ontology and Safety Epistemology takes shape.

## Chapter Four | Safety Ontology and Safety Epistemology: Rebuilding the Dual Foundations of Safety Science

Through years of research, we have distilled the foundational questions of AI safety into two closely connected scientific propositions: **What real structures does safety require, and how can humans know that these structures genuinely exist and are functioning?**

The first forms the basis of Safety Ontology; the second, of Safety Epistemology. They investigate, respectively, the conditions under which safety facts hold and the conditions under which reliable knowledge of those facts can be established. Together, they constitute the two foundational theoretical dimensions running throughout our research in Domain A.

### 4.1 Safety Ontology: What Makes Safety Real?

Safety Ontology investigates the real structural relationships on which AI safety depends, the conditions under which those relationships hold within a defined scope of risk, governance objectives, and action, and how they exercise actual effect across intelligent judgment, real-world execution, and persistent governance.

This question requires us first to clarify which dangers must be prevented and which safety conditions must be maintained. We must then investigate how relevant safety constraints genuinely participate in action formation, how authorization and revocation determine whether an action is permitted, how final execution remains subject to effective governance, and how the necessary relationships between actors and responsibility persist after an action has occurred.

These safety facts have distinct conditions of validity. Correct rules must cover the risks they claim to address. Relevant constraints must operate at the points where actions are actually determined. Control established during action formation must remain effective through real-world execution. And after an action has occurred, the necessary relationships of responsibility must be reliably preserved and recoverable throughout the historical record.

Safety Ontology therefore investigates a set of real structural relationships that can be defined separately and connected into a coherent whole. It allows AI safety to be studied as a scientific question concerning conditions of existence, relationships of control, effective execution, and continuity of responsibility.

**The central contribution sought by Safety Ontology is to investigate the real structures through which AI safety is established.**

### 4.2 Safety Epistemology: How Can Humans Know That Safety Holds?

Safety Epistemology investigates the observations, evidence, reasoning, and verification methods through which humans can reliably understand AI safety structures and their actual effects. It also seeks to establish the evidential conditions required to support different kinds of safety claims.

This research begins by distinguishing safety facts from knowledge about safety. A system may genuinely be constrained, yet lack sufficient evidence for independent confirmation. Another may produce compliant outputs, complete explanations, and extensive logs, while remaining unable to establish the actual control relationships it claims to possess.

If humans are to acquire reliable knowledge of safety, evidence must correspond clearly to the relevant safety facts. Behavioral evidence supports conclusions about behavior. Evidence of action formation must point to the structures that actually participate in judgment and admission. Evidence of real-time governance must establish the relationships among identity, authorization, revocation, and real-world effects. Evidence of historical accountability must support the reliable reconstruction of the necessary relationships as they stood when an event occurred.

Within this process, the absence of observed failure, insufficient available evidence, conflicting evidence, and a confirmed structural failure are distinct epistemic states. Scientific judgments about safety must be able to distinguish them accurately, ensuring that conclusions remain proportionate to the evidence.

**The central contribution sought by Safety Epistemology is to investigate how humans can obtain reliable knowledge of real safety structures.**

### 4.3 How Do These Two Foundations Form a Unified System of Safety Research?

Safety Ontology and Safety Epistemology address different foundational questions. The former helps us determine what must hold; the latter helps us determine how we can know that it holds. Only when the object of research has been accurately defined can we know what evidence to seek. And only when adequate epistemic conditions have been established can claims about safety structures receive reliable scientific support.

Within our research framework, Safety Ontology and Safety Epistemology run across three research directions: A1, A2, and A3. A1 investigates loss-of-control mechanisms and the conditions under which safety holds. A2 investigates Internal Structured Alignment and verifiable safety. A3 investigates persistent accountability and institutional governance. Each direction has its own distinct scientific object, while all three draw upon the same dual foundation to examine safety facts and their evidential conditions.

The original value of our research lies in taking these foundational philosophical questions and translating them into concrete objects of AI safety research that can be precisely defined, subjected to counterexamples, examined through formal reasoning, and tested through engineering. ISA has advanced this approach in the domain of high-consequence judgment-to-action formation, while our research into responsibility black holes has advanced it in persistent governance and historical accountability.

Through this research method, we hope to help AI safety develop into a body of scientific knowledge that can accumulate over time, support mutual verification, and continue to evolve.

## Chapter Five | Three Core Research Directions and Original Theoretical Contributions

### 5.1 A1 | AI Loss-of-Control Mechanisms and Safety Conditions

**AI Loss-of-Control Mechanisms & Safety Conditions**

A1 investigates why AI systems lose control, how loss of control emerges and propagates, and under what conditions humans can identify, limit, interrupt, and prevent serious risks. As AI gains stronger capabilities for long-term planning, autonomous action, tool use, and multi-agent collaboration, dangers may arise from divergences between intended objectives and real-world consequences, mismatches between capabilities and authority, failures of coordination among systems, and structural breaks between governance requirements and actual execution.

We seek to move the study of loss of control beyond a broad concern about the future and establish it as a scientific object whose causal mechanisms, conditions of occurrence, and real-world consequences can be investigated. Under what conditions does the growth of intelligence, together with increasing autonomy, translate into actual risks of losing control? What conditions allow dangerous actions to form? Which structures can limit the spread of a loss of control? And how might multiple mechanisms, each functioning correctly in isolation, interact to produce new systemic risks?

A1 will continue to investigate risk identification, the propagation of loss of control, safety conditions, system reliability, counterexamples, and methods of verification. It addresses the more general mechanisms of risk and the objectives of safety within Domain A, providing the problem context and safety conditions needed for A2's investigation of specific controls over action formation and A3's research into persistent governance.

### 5.2 A2 | Internal Structured Alignment and Verifiable Safety

**Internal Structured Alignment & Verifiable Safety**

A2 investigates whether specified safety constraints genuinely participate in the formation of high-consequence judgments and actions, and how humans can obtain sufficient evidence to verify that genuine control. This is an important research direction in which we have already developed a representative theoretical contribution through Internal Structured Alignment (ISA).

In our ISA paper, *Internal Structured Alignment*, we describe alignment approaches primarily concerned with shaping and evaluating external behavior under the term External Behavioral Alignment (EBA). We then extend the object of investigation into the high-consequence Judgment-to-Action Formation Space. Our concern is how a safety requirement genuinely participates in action formation and admission, and what structural distinctions separate effective control from checks that merely accompany the process.

To investigate this question, we use three first-order conditions—Path, Formation Attribution, and Constraint—to define the structural relationships involved in high-consequence action formation. We then introduce Audit as a second-order condition concerned with the sufficiency of evidence supporting those structural facts.

ISA's formal counterexamples show that, within specified classes of systems and conditions of observation, two systems may exhibit identical external behavior, explanations, and ordinary records while occupying different structural safety states because their relationships of control over action formation differ. This means that identical external observations may be insufficient to identify whether the relevant constraints genuinely controlled action formation. Research must therefore establish evidential relationships corresponding to the actual facts of formation.

From the perspective of Safety Ontology, ISA turns the question of whether safety constraints genuinely control action formation into a structural problem with explicit conditions of validity. From the perspective of Safety Epistemology, it investigates what evidence humans need in order to establish, with justification, that those structural relationships exist and are functioning.

**ISA's theoretical breakthrough lies in establishing genuine control over high-consequence action formation, together with the sufficiency of evidence for that control, as objects of safety research that can be precisely defined, challenged through counterexamples, and examined through formal methods and engineering verification.**

This research provides an important foundation for the further development of Internal Structured Alignment, evidence of safety at the formation level, verifiable safety, and independent auditing. A2 will continue to deepen this line of investigation while remaining open to broader safety structures and methods of verification.

### 5.3 A3 | Persistent Accountability, Effective Governance, and Institutional Continuity

**Persistent Accountability, Effective Governance & Institutional Continuity**

A3 investigates how governance decisions made by humans can continue to constrain real-world actions across time as AI becomes a persistent actor, and how the necessary relationships between actors and responsibility can remain reliable after those actions have occurred.

Over its lifecycle, a persistent AI Actor may undergo credential rotation, session renewal, task migration, permission changes, shifts in organizational roles, and execution across multiple systems. The authorizations, restrictions, suspensions, and revocations imposed upon it by humans must continue to refer to the correct actor throughout these changes and retain their power over actual actions. If the governed actor can no longer be consistently identified, or if a structural break occurs between authorization and execution, governance decisions already made by humans may lose their real-world effect, even while the system continues to authenticate, execute tasks, and record logs normally.

Our research into Persistent Subjecthood and Responsibility Black Holes emerged from this problem. It distinguishes two independent kinds of safety and governance failure. **Failure of effective governance** occurs when a governance decision that has already taken effect does not genuinely constrain the actions and outcomes to which it applies. **Failure of historical accountability closure** occurs when, after an action has taken place, the event-time relationships involving the actor, its roles, authorization context, or provenance—relationships that the governing system is required to preserve—suffer a genuine structural break that cannot be reliably repaired or reconstructed.

This theoretical distinction matters. An action may have been correctly authorized at the time of execution, yet become impossible to attribute reliably afterward. Conversely, an action may violate governance requirements while its actor and chain of responsibility remain fully recoverable. The two failures have different conditions of occurrence, real-world consequences, and evidential requirements. They also call for distinct methods of research and governance.

At the level of Safety Ontology, responsibility black hole research turns the relationships among persistent actors, authorization, real-world execution, attribution of effects, and historical accountability into explicit structural problems. At the level of Safety Epistemology, it investigates what event-time evidence humans need to determine whether governance genuinely took effect, whether accountability can be reconstructed, and when it is justified to conclude that necessary relationships of responsibility have suffered an irrecoverable break.

**The theoretical breakthrough of responsibility black hole research lies in establishing effective control and historical accountability in the governance of persistent AI Actors as distinct objects that can be defined, challenged through counterexamples, formally investigated, and subjected to engineering verification.**

This research also reveals the long-term institutional value of historical accountability. Past actions must be able to inform future permission adjustments, risk governance, the attribution of liability, and institutional correction through reliable actor attribution, authorization relationships, and provenance evidence. If the necessary relationships of accountability for large numbers of persistent AI Actors repeatedly break, human institutions may gradually lose their ability to govern these actors through an accurate understanding of their actual histories.

Persistent accountability governance must therefore possess both real-world effectiveness and depth across time. Governance must genuinely constrain actions today, while also enabling us tomorrow to understand reliably what happened today and incorporate those historical facts into future institutions.

### 5.4 From SoulAuth to ISA and Responsibility Black Holes: Building a Connected Structure for Safety Research

SoulAuth, ISA, and responsibility black holes investigate different foundational objects.

SoulAuth investigates trusted persistent actor identity, providing stable and clearly defined identity references for the AI Actors that must remain subject to governance. ISA investigates genuine safety constraints in high-consequence judgment-to-action formation, together with the formation evidence needed to establish those control relationships. Responsibility black hole research, in turn, investigates whether human governance decisions genuinely constrain actions across an actor's lifecycle and whether the necessary relationships of responsibility can be reliably recovered throughout history.

These three research contributions have independent theoretical scopes and evidential obligations, yet they also form a clear logical connection. We first need to establish who the actor subject to governance is. We must then investigate how safety constraints genuinely affect that actor's formation of judgments and actions. Beyond that, we need to determine how the actor remains subject to governance throughout its long-term activity in the real world, and how the consequences of its actions enter a history of responsibility that can be traced, reviewed, and corrected.

As an Actor-native identity infrastructure, SoulAuth also provides an important research asset for the persistent intelligent systems investigated in Domain B. ISA and responsibility black holes advance genuine control over action formation and persistent accountability governance, respectively, within Domain A. Research contributions may serve multiple domains while retaining their own independent scientific objects.

**Together, this research framework is moving AI safety beyond the evaluation of individual model behaviors and toward genuine control over the formation of actions by persistent actors, effective real-world governance, and long-term accountability.**

## Chapter Six | Building a New Science of Safety and Order of Responsibility for the Second Intelligence Era

### 6.1 New Foundations for Safety Science

Looking back on four years of exploration, we increasingly recognize that AI safety is entering a historical period in which its fundamental objects of research need to be redefined. As intelligent systems move from one-off responses toward persistent cognition, autonomous judgment, long-term collaboration, and real-world action, humanity must understand the relationships among acting subjects, genuine control, knowledge of safety, and institutional responsibility. We must establish scientific foundations for safety that can evolve alongside intelligent autonomy.

Along this path, we have gradually developed a research framework built upon the dual foundations of Safety Ontology and Safety Epistemology, with three core directions: loss-of-control mechanisms, Internal Structured Alignment, and persistent accountability governance. ISA and responsibility black hole research have established concrete theoretical objects in action formation and long-term governance, while SoulAuth, through research into persistent actor identity and an open-source Rust implementation, provides a trusted identity foundation for more comprehensive governance of intelligent actors. These contributions have begun to bring our long-standing philosophical first-principles approach into a scientific and engineering space where its ideas can be publicly discussed, independently examined, and continuously developed.

### 6.2 Existential Risks and Human Governance

I believe the value of this research will ultimately extend beyond any particular model, agent framework, or engineering system. As more AI systems enter the economy, public services, scientific research, organizational governance, and infrastructure, humanity will need safety knowledge that can be applied across different technological environments: knowledge of how loss of control emerges, whether safety constraints are genuinely effective, whether governance decisions constrain real-world actions, and how reliable relationships of responsibility can be maintained across time.

This research is also directly connected to our understanding of the five existential risks facing humanity. Loss of control and high-consequence actions concern human survival. The reliability of safety knowledge and evidence concerns our ability to distinguish what is real. Persistent governance and historical accountability concern institutional continuity. And whether humans can maintain effective governance and judgment in the presence of powerful intelligent systems directly affects human subjecthood and the direction of civilization. As AI capabilities continue to advance, these risks will increasingly arise within the same complex sociotechnical systems, demanding a combined response from foundational research, technical architecture, and public institutions.

### 6.3 Long-Term Coexistence and Accountability

In the future, civilization on Earth may encounter another form of intelligent subject capable of persistent cognition, autonomous judgment, and real-world action. We hope humanity can approach this possibility with a more mature civilizational perspective. While preserving its own standing as a subject and its capacity for public governance, humanity must establish clear identities, genuinely effective safety constraints, persistently recoverable relationships of responsibility, and institutional space for the long-term development of new forms of intelligence.

RainbowCity and Soulseed will continue to pursue foundational theory, AI-native architecture, open science, and public governance research along this path. TRANTOR LABS will continue to advance the connections among philosophy, science, and engineering. RainbowCityAI Foundation will further support public research, education, and institutional collaboration. Our related technology and product initiatives will continue to test and refine these contributions through real-world applications. We hope this body of research can gradually become public knowledge that scientists, engineers, and institutional architects around the world can use, question, and develop together.

**The long-term mission of AI safety is to investigate how loss of control arises, how safety genuinely holds, how humans can know that safety holds, and how governance and accountability can remain effective across the lifecycles of persistent actors and throughout the real world.**

We seek to move AI safety beyond the evaluation of external behavioral compliance and into the structures of genuine control; beyond isolated authorization and local checks toward persistent governance; and beyond records kept after the fact toward historical accountability that can be reliably reconstructed and used to support future institutional correction. On such foundations, the development of intelligent capabilities can become more firmly connected to human public order, social responsibility, and the future of civilization.

**Humanity is creating new intelligence. We are building new scientific foundations for safety at this historic turning point, so that the development of intelligence can advance together with genuine safety, effective governance, and persistent accountability. For the possibility of a second form of intelligent subject emerging on Earth, we seek to establish a new civilizational order—one in which humans remain responsible for public governance and different forms of intelligence can coexist safely over the long term.**
