## Preface | Four Years of Exploration: Discovering Another Civilizational Crisis

Hello, I'm KunYuan.

For the past four years, my team and I have been exploring the long-term existence, native cognition, safety, and governance of artificial intelligence in Singapore. What began as an AI soulmate venture gradually led us into foundational research on persistent intelligent actors, identity security, the formation of judgment, and accountability. Through theoretical and architectural work on ISA, responsibility black holes, SoulAuth, and MindOS, we have sought to establish real and effective safety constraints for increasingly powerful AI.

Yet while studying how to prevent AI from escaping human control, I gradually came to see another, far less visible civilizational crisis. It may require no hostility from machines and no war between AI and humanity. On the contrary, it may unfold as AI becomes more reliable, society more efficient, and human life more convenient. As we increasingly entrust the formation of our understanding, judgments, and direction to external intelligence, we may gain unprecedented capabilities while taking less and less part in shaping our own lives.

In June 2026, I brought this question into academic research through the paper [*The Risk-Bearing Subject of AI-Related Existential Risk to Humanity*](https://zenodo.org/records/20728025). It examines whether, beyond direct threats to survival, humanity could face another form of existential risk if the conditions that make human subjecthood possible within civilization were systematically damaged. I then began bringing this question into everyday thought and life through *The Author of Our Life* and the RainbowCity trilogy on human subjecthood. Alongside this work, my team and I have been developing Human Judgment Preservation (HJP), with the aim of giving the protection of human judgment a practical foundation in products and engineering.

In “Afterword II | The Civilizational Fork” of *The Author of Our Life*, I proposed a thought experiment about the next ten years: If AI continues to succeed, and civilization simply follows the momentum of efficiency and automation, where will humanity eventually find itself?

That thought experiment has strengthened my conviction that **the Second Intelligence Era calls for a civilizational principle on which we cannot retreat: however far intelligence advances, human beings must not disappear from the process by which their own lives take shape.**

This is the starting point of RainbowCity and Soulseed's third long-term research domain. Domain A examines what makes AI safety real. Domain B investigates how persistent intelligence can develop a native mind and a governable runtime architecture. **Domain C turns the inquiry back toward humanity itself: as another kind of intelligence enters our world, how can human beings remain the authors of their own lives and civilizational future?**

Through this research, I hope to help bring about a new enlightenment for the Second Intelligence Era, inviting scientists, thinkers, educators, engineers, and institution builders to explore and build a new civilizational order in which humanity and a possible future form of intelligence can coexist over the long term.

## Chapter One | As AI Grows More Powerful, What Might Humanity Lose?

### 1.1 From Threats to Survival to a Crisis of Civilizational Subjecthood

The risk of AI escaping human control has become an unavoidable subject of modern technological safety research. As intelligent systems gain stronger planning, tool-use, and real-world action capabilities, misdirected goals, failed permissions, and dangerous operations may cause serious harm, potentially threatening human survival and the basic order of society. We need safety science, native architectures, and effective institutions so that consequential actions are genuinely constrained and responsibility can be reconstructed after an accident.

But suppose AI never suffers a catastrophic loss of control. Suppose it develops with extraordinary success. Would humanity then be assured of a safe civilizational future?

A civilization's continued existence depends on more than its members remaining alive. It also depends on their ability to understand a shared world, form judgments, sustain institutions, bear responsibility, create meaning, and continue deciding their future. If those abilities and the conditions through which they develop are systematically weakened over time, civilization may lose something fundamental even while society remains prosperous.

We must therefore examine different pathways of harm. On one hand, increasingly powerful AI may threaten human survival through dangerous actions. On the other, in the course of sustained collaboration with AI, human beings may gradually lose the conditions that allow them to remain subjects within their own civilization. The latter may have no identifiable accident scene. It may emerge precisely when everything appears to be improving.

### 1.2 When Capabilities Grow but the Formation of Judgment Moves Elsewhere

Today, AI helps us read, write, analyze, learn, make decisions, and organize our lives. It can turn complex material into clear explanations, compare alternatives rapidly, and offer increasingly well-developed recommendations tailored to individual needs. These capabilities genuinely expand what human beings can accomplish. At the same time, they are beginning to change how we participate in forming judgments.

A person may retain the final decision without having truly participated in forming the judgment behind it. An organization may preserve an approval procedure while handing almost all problem interpretation, evidence selection, and option development to intelligent systems. As these patterns spread through education, work, and public life, people may become more adept at using intelligence while gaining less experience in how important judgments emerge from uncertain reality.

**A civilization may gain unprecedented intellectual capabilities while gradually losing the conditions through which it decides what those capabilities are for.** This less visible risk deserves serious study because it may ultimately affect humanity's ability to continue understanding, judging, and collectively governing its own civilization.

## Chapter Two | The Silent Drift of an Efficiency-Driven Civilization: When Local Rationality Changes the Whole

### 2.1 A Thought Experiment About 2036

In “Afterword II | The Civilizational Fork,” I invited readers into a thought experiment. Let us begin in 2026. For the moment, set aside AI rebellion and assume no major technological failure. Instead, suppose AI advances steadily over the next decade: models become more reliable, long-term memory improves, and intelligent agents take on increasingly complex work, becoming deeply involved in education, medicine, social organizations, and individual lives.

Now let us allow the most familiar logic of our present civilization to keep running. Whatever can become more efficient should become more efficient. Whatever can cost less should cost less. Whatever can be automated should be automated further.

A company wants higher productivity; a school wants students to learn faster; a hospital wants fewer errors; a government wants to deliver more accurate public services. All of these aims are reasonable. For individuals, letting AI read extensive materials, compare complex options, and handle tedious tasks can genuinely improve life. I use AI extensively myself and have long been engaged in building intelligent systems. Precisely because its benefits are so real, I have come to understand that this crisis requires neither a designer intent on stripping humanity of subjecthood nor a single, obviously mistaken technological decision.

I call this structure **local rationality**. Each participant makes reasonable choices from where they stand. Yet when countless local choices keep moving toward the same goal of efficiency, civilization as a whole may arrive at a destination no one ever consciously chose.

### 2.2 From Delegating Capabilities to Relocating the Formation of Judgment

Technology first took over repetitive labor and mechanical operations. Such tasks consume time without necessarily contributing much to how we develop as subjects. As AI advanced, we began asking it to organize material, explain problems, analyze evidence, and compare alternatives. As intelligent systems became more reliable, that delegation naturally extended inward, toward the formation of cognition itself. AI no longer merely helps us carry out a defined task; it also begins to determine which problems deserve attention, which facts enter our field of view, which options seem more valuable, and which direction appears likely to produce a better outcome.

What makes this shift so difficult to notice is that it comes with persuasive reasons of efficiency at every step. The better AI performs, the less immediate reason we have to take these processes back. A person can obtain high-quality judgments with increasing ease while gradually ceasing to experience parts of the process through which those judgments once took shape.

We can, of course, retain Human-in-the-loop arrangements: consequential actions still require human confirmation, and high-risk operations still require authorization. But judgment begins long before final approval. It begins when a problem is framed, evidence is selected, and particular values are given weight. If those processes have largely been completed before a person is asked to accept or reject the outcome, the person may remain in the workflow while the formation of judgment moves elsewhere.

**Being in the loop does not mean that one's judgment is still taking part in the process.** The real research question, then, is whether people retain the ability to re-enter, question, change, and take responsibility as AI assistance reaches further into the formation of problems, reasons, and values.

### 2.3 The Next Generation: A Loss That Does Not Feel Like Loss

The deepest part of this transformation may not concern today's adults. Our generation has at least experienced a world without the constant presence of mature AI. We know what it is to face an unfamiliar question without knowing where to begin, to read difficult material repeatedly, and to arrive at an important judgment only after engaging with reality, conflict, and time. Not every difficulty is worth preserving, but some of these experiences have genuinely taught us how to ask questions, organize reasons, live with uncertainty, and form judgments we can stand behind.

Children of the future may grow up from their earliest years in an environment where intelligent systems can explain knowledge, correct errors, organize arguments, and plan learning paths at any moment. For them, AI may not be a tool that entered their lives later. It may be part of the world in which they first learn to make sense of things.

The question therefore changes. It is no longer simply whether people use certain abilities less often; it is whether the next generation will have sufficient opportunity to develop them in the first place. Someone who has never fully experienced particular processes of forming judgment may not feel that anything has been lost, because there is no earlier life against which to compare it. **The hardest loss to recognize may be the abilities that never had a full chance to take shape.** When such changes extend across generations, the issue of subjecthood moves beyond individual technological dependence and becomes a question of what civilization passes on.

### 2.4 From Social Momentum to Civilizational Path Dependence

As AI raises productivity, schools, companies, public institutions, and systems of social evaluation may reorganize around new levels of efficiency. Processes that require time, independent thought, and uncertainty may increasingly be treated as unnecessary costs. A person who understands the value of independent judgment may still struggle to find opportunities to develop it again; schools and organizations that care about subjecthood may nonetheless face pressures of short-term performance and competition.

Over time, this may create a form of path dependence. Civilization develops not only new technological habits but also a different environment for cultivating and sustaining the ability to remain a subject in one's own life. If taken to an extreme, people may continue working, living, creating, and enjoying prosperity while taking an ever smaller part in shaping their own lives and collective future.

In *The Civilizational Fork*, I describe this possible endpoint as **the death of human subjecthood**, or **silent death**. There need be no clear moment of war or catastrophe. Civilization might be more prosperous than ever even as it loses the conditions that sustain human subjecthood. The year 2036 serves as a thought experiment through which to examine today's choices, not as a certain prediction of the future. It brings into view a civilizational mechanism worth studying: **if gains in efficiency are consistently rewarded while no one is responsible for preserving the conditions in which subjecthood develops, civilization may accumulate a form of damage that short-term measures cannot easily detect, even as it continues to advance.**

## Chapter Three | Human Subjecthood: How Can We Remain the Authors of Our Own Lives?

### 3.1 Subjecthood and Our Participation in Shaping Our Lives

In *The Author of Our Life*, I have continually returned to one question: How does a person become the author of their own life?

Subjecthood includes the freedom to choose and the capacity to understand reality, evaluate reasons, form judgments, and bear the consequences. But what matters is not only what someone ultimately chooses; it is also how that choice takes shape. We need to be able to ask questions, understand which facts matter, weigh competing values and costs, and revise our judgments through experience. Our lives are always shaped by our bodies, cultures, relationships, and social conditions. No one can determine everything alone. Yet through understanding, responding, and choosing, we can still take part in shaping the lives we live.

What I mean by subjecthood, therefore, includes both a person's genuine participation in forming the important questions, judgments, and directions of their life, and the living and social conditions that allow that capacity to continue developing. It does not require everyone to perform every task personally. Reasonable delegation can itself express independent judgment. What must be preserved is the opportunity to understand, question, reframe, and change direction when important matters arise.

This is also the heart of **cognitive sovereignty**: even when drawing on external intelligence, a person continues to take a genuine part in shaping the understanding and judgments they call their own.

### 3.2 Human Judgment: Preserving the Power to Form, Not Merely to Approve

As AI enters complex forms of collaboration, human oversight has become an important principle of governance. But protecting subjecthood requires a further distinction between the authority to approve a final action and the ability to participate in forming the judgment itself. A system may lawfully require a person to approve an operation without providing enough context to understand the issue. It may allow objections without letting those objections actually change what happens next. Human participation exists procedurally, yet lacks the corresponding power to shape the process and its outcome.

To address this problem, we have developed Human Judgment Preservation (HJP), identifying five dimensions of human judgment: **Frame**, the ability to define and redefine the problem; **Evaluate**, to assess evidence, alternatives, and costs; **Choose**, to form a choice of direction; **Intervene**, to question, modify, pause, or refuse; and **Own & Learn**, to stand behind one's judgment and continue learning from its consequences.

These dimensions let us study how a person actually participates in judgment, rather than merely counting approvals. Protecting judgment does not mean forcing people to repeat work at every step. Low-risk, well-defined, reversible tasks can reasonably be delegated to AI. Where important life directions, major value trade-offs, or consequential actions are involved, however, people need genuine opportunities to understand, participate, and change the course of events.

We have carried these principles into formal HJP protocols and product-experience design, guided by a simple rule: **Do not interrupt by default. Make important matters understandable. Make change possible when needed. Show what happened afterward.** Subsequent research and engineering validation will continue to examine whether human judgments were genuinely formed, handled appropriately, and able to affect later outcomes. Evidence of participation in a particular collaboration must never be used to pass judgment on a person's overall intelligence, character, or subjecthood.

### 3.3 From Individual Ability to the Conditions of Civilization

Subjecthood has both an individual dimension and a social and institutional one. A book may help someone rediscover the importance of independent judgment. But if everyday products offer only final answers, schools assess only completed results, and organizations reward ever greater automation, a person may still find it difficult to take part in how judgments are formed.

Products must therefore allow people to return to the formation of questions and criteria. Education must give children opportunities to ask their own questions and develop reasons. Organizations must allow meaningful objections to change decisions, while public institutions should examine how to preserve the real relationship between human judgment and responsibility.

**One of civilization's most important inheritances is the opportunity for the next generation to keep forming knowledge, asking questions, and deciding the future.** At this point, human subjecthood becomes a question for civilizational research, not only a matter of personal philosophy.

## Chapter Four | From Species Survival to Civilizational Conditions: Rethinking Existential Risk

### 4.1 One Human Species, Different Pathways of Existential Harm

In June 2026, we published *The Risk-Bearing Subject of AI-Related Existential Risk to Humanity: From Species Survival to the Realization of Subjecthood under Civilizational Conditions*. One of its central questions is who bears AI-related existential risk, and what must be damaged for a risk to have existential significance.

The risk-bearing subject remains humanity. Yet the continued existence of human beings as subjects of a civilization across generations depends on more than biological survival. It also depends on the fundamental conditions through which we recognize shared reality, build institutions, form judgments, bear responsibility, and create meaning. We therefore ask whether AI might inflict long-term, systemic harm upon those conditions through pathways other than the direct destruction of the human species.

This question broadens the scope of existential risk that can be studied and gives human subjecthood, cognitive mediation, and civilizational transmission a more precise theoretical place.

### 4.2 When Does an Individual Change Become a Civilizational Existential Risk?

Not every act of cognitive delegation damages subjecthood. Nor does a temporary decline in one person's independent thinking establish that humanity is facing an existential crisis. To assess whether a risk rises to the level of civilization, we must examine the object of harm, the mechanisms of its spread, its duration, and the conditions of recovery. Does it damage the foundations on which human beings continue to form judgments? Can it extend beyond particular tasks into education, organizations, and public institutions? Could it accumulate across generations into structural harm that is difficult to reverse? And could it ultimately undermine humanity's collective ability to understand and govern civilization as a subject oriented toward its own future?

*The Civilizational Fork* portrays an extreme possibility: the hollowing out of human subjecthood. Existential-risk research must go further, investigating the conditions under which such a possibility might arise, the mechanisms that could prevent it, and the evidence through which we might assess its development. This is a long-term program of theoretical and empirical inquiry for Domain C.

### 4.3 Five Existential Risks and the Foundations of Civilization

In RainbowCity and Soulseed's long-term civilizational research, we organize the fundamental risks facing humanity into five interconnected dimensions: **survival, reality, institutional continuity, subjecthood, and meaning**.

Survival risk concerns whether humanity can continue living safely and sustain basic social order. Reality risk concerns whether we can continue recognizing and verifying a shared world. Institutional-continuity risk concerns whether public authority, accountability, and governance remain effective over time. Subjecthood risk concerns whether human beings can still form their own questions, judgments, and directions. Meaning risk concerns whether people retain the conditions for creating, choosing, and giving shape to meaning in a highly intelligent civilization.

These risks are deeply connected. A society unable to establish shared facts will struggle increasingly with public judgment. Institutions that cannot sustain accountability may lose the ability to govern consequential actions. If the conditions that allow subjecthood to develop are steadily damaged, humanity may also find it harder to recognize dangers, reform institutions, and change its civilizational direction.

Domain C examines these questions through the conditions of human civilization as a whole, while remaining connected to Domain A's safety science and Domain B's AI-native systems architecture. We seek a fuller understanding of existential risk: **one that asks not only whether humanity can survive, but whether humanity can continue to exist as a civilizational subject.**

## Chapter Five | The Civilizational Fork: A New Enlightenment for the Second Intelligence Era

### 5.1 Modern Civilization and the Historical Emergence of Human Subjecthood

The development of modern civilization is closely connected to the gradual recognition of human beings as subjects capable of reason, freedom, rights, and participation in public life. From humanism and the Enlightenment through the development of science, the rule of law, and democratic institutions, humanity has undergone long and complex transformations in thought and governance. Thinkers, scientists, and social reformers across civilizations and historical periods have worked to expand people's ability to use reason to understand the world, question authority, bear responsibility, and help determine the terms of their shared life.

These efforts were not completed at a single moment, and modern civilization has by no means fully realized everyone's subjecthood. Yet one of its major achievements has been to widen the possibilities for human beings to participate in the world as rational and public subjects.

Today, AI is changing the conditions under which people understand the world and form judgments. In earlier struggles, human beings sought freedom from the one-way domination of thought by external authorities. Now we also face an external intelligence capable of continuously providing persuasive explanations, recommendations, and decisions. It need not prohibit independent thinking to change our habits of asking questions and forming reasons. Safeguarding subjecthood in the age of AI therefore calls for a renewed response in both thought and institutions.

### 5.2 What Kind of New Enlightenment Does the Second Intelligence Era Need?

I hope to help bring about a new enlightenment for the Second Intelligence Era—one that encourages humanity to recognize why the ability to form judgment still matters, and how technology, education, and institutions can preserve the conditions in which that ability develops as more powerful intelligence enters our lives.

This enlightenment does not ask humanity to return to a world without AI. On the contrary, we want AI to grow stronger, take on more mechanical labor, expand scientific exploration and creative possibilities, and deepen its collaboration with people. But when AI helps someone understand the world, it should expand that person's capacity to understand. When it helps an organization make a mature decision, it should leave the organization better able to comprehend, question, and take responsibility for the outcome. When it supports the next generation's learning, it should create more opportunities for independent cognition to take shape.

**A new enlightenment would put increasingly powerful intelligence in the service of human development, while allowing humanity to keep deciding what those capabilities are for.** This idea must find its way into social practice. Individual awareness alone cannot indefinitely counter the momentum of efficiency, cost reduction, and competition.

### 5.3 Two Civilizational Paths, Both with Powerful AI

This is why I propose **the civilizational fork**. The fork is between two possible futures that both possess powerful AI but organize social life around different highest principles.

The first path continues to be driven chiefly by efficiency, automation, cost, and the quality of outcomes. AI may grow ever more powerful, and people may receive ever better services. But if the formation of judgment and the development of subjecthood have no independent place among the goals being protected, human participation may drift steadily toward the final approval of outcomes. The result could be an extraordinarily prosperous civilization in which human subjecthood is gradually hollowed out.

The second path releases the same immense potential of AI while deliberately making the continued formation of human subjecthood a civilizational principle that cannot be surrendered. It pursues efficiency while also protecting the conditions through which people form judgments in education, work, public deliberation, and important life choices. Technological progress and human self-development can then proceed together.

The difference lies in what civilization understands its purpose to be. Efficiency, growth, and intellectual capability have tremendous value—but what should they serve? When technology can make increasingly good choices on our behalf, do human beings still need to participate in deciding what is worth pursuing? When AI can do more and more work, do we still need to preserve opportunities for the next generation to ask questions, form judgments, and create meaning?

I hope our civilization will make a deliberate choice: **preserving the conditions in which human subjecthood can continue to develop must be among the highest principles of the Second Intelligence Era. Advances in technological capability and efficiency should serve the possibility of human beings remaining the authors of their own lives and civilization.**

Choosing this path means choosing a more mature relationship of coexistence: distinct kinds of intelligence can create together while humanity retains the real capacity to determine its own civilizational direction.

## Chapter Six | From Awakening Ideas to Building Civilization: Research and Public Action

### 6.1 Three Distinct but Connected Research Directions

To develop this civilizational question into sustained scientific inquiry, philosophical work, and public practice, we organize Domain C into three research directions.

**C1 | Human Subjecthood, Cognitive Sovereignty, and the Formation of Judgment.** This direction examines how people can retain the real conditions for framing problems, evaluating evidence, choosing directions, and owning their judgments when AI is deeply involved in cognition. It begins with philosophical research into authorship of life and cognitive sovereignty, and extends into HJP, human–AI collaboration, and mechanisms for preserving subjecthood.

**C2 | AI Existential Risk and the Civilizational Conditions of Human Subjecthood.** This direction studies the different pathways through which the same human species may suffer existential harm. It asks how the foundations of civilization may be affected by AI-related technological and institutional change, and under what conditions cognitive delegation, erosion of subjecthood, and risks to intergenerational transmission acquire civilizational significance. Our June 2026 existential-risk paper is an important public starting point for this work.

**C3 | The Civilizational Fork, a New Enlightenment, and Multi-Intelligence Coexistence.** This direction studies how humanity can deliberately choose its future path through the circulation of ideas, education, technological design, public institutions, and international collaboration. Its goal is to protect the conditions in which human subjecthood continues to develop while building an order of safety, responsibility, and long-term coexistence for a possible future form of intelligence.

These three directions ask what humanity must protect, what mechanisms might damage those conditions, and how we can deliberately change the future.

### 6.2 The RainbowCity Trilogy on Human Subjecthood: Rethinking Civilization Through Everyday Life

I began writing the RainbowCity trilogy on human subjecthood because I wanted ordinary people to see a transformation that could affect their own lives, the next generation, and the future of civilization.

The first volume, *The Author of Our Life: Protecting Our Cognitive Sovereignty in the Age of AI*, begins with everyday cognition and lived experience. It explores how people understand their own judgments, choices, and authorship in the age of AI. Its “Afterword II | The Civilizational Fork” carries the question of personal subjecthood forward into a choice about civilization's future direction.

The second volume, *Rebuilding Our Cognitive OS*, goes further into how human cognition takes shape and can be rebuilt, asking how we come to understand the world anew within the conditions of body, language, culture, education, and society. The planned third work, *Keep Judgment in the Loop*, continues toward the questions of life's direction, value judgments, and collaboration that preserves subjecthood. Together, the trilogy traces a path from knowing ourselves, to understanding how we are formed, to deciding our life's direction anew.

Through these works, we hope more people will see that cognitive sovereignty concerns both how individuals live and whether civilization can continue cultivating subjects capable of independent judgment and creating the future.

### 6.3 Bringing Philosophy into Research, Education, Products, and Institutions

Books and public ideas can help people recognize the problem. But if our technological and social environments continue along their existing path, individual awakening may have little room to become effective action. We therefore hope to advance open research alongside practical efforts to build new conditions.

In academic research, we invite philosophers, cognitive scientists, social scientists, education researchers, and AI safety researchers to study the real relationships among cognitive delegation, participation in judgment, intergenerational development, and civilizational risk. Papers, experiments, longitudinal studies, and public academic conferences can make these questions part of a body of knowledge open to testing, criticism, and continued development.

In product research, we have established formal HJP designs and protocols and hope to develop lightweight tools, plugins, and open components that protect human judgment—so people can understand what matters, change course when necessary, and know how their judgment affected what happened next. In education, we want the next generation to benefit fully from AI's knowledge and capabilities while still having opportunities to ask independent questions, organize reasons, confront uncertainty, and form judgments of their own.

At the institutional level, organizations, schools, companies, and governments need to study how to establish the conditions for genuine human participation in consequential judgments, bringing the benefits of automation and the protection of human autonomy into social governance together. All of this requires open knowledge and broad collaboration.

**Only when the protection of subjecthood becomes a shared responsibility of thought, science, technology, and public institutions can another civilizational path gain a durable foundation.**

## Chapter Seven | Building a Shared Civilizational Order for Humanity and a Possible Second Form of Intelligence

### 7.1 How Can Two Kinds of Intelligence Coexist in One World?

As artificial intelligence continues to advance, Earth may one day see the emergence of another kind of intelligent subject, capable of maintaining a persistent identity, developing its own cognition, and acting in the real world. If that day comes, what order of identity, action, and responsibility should humanity establish for it? How can it have a reasonable space for autonomy while human safety, public governance, and civilizational direction remain protected? When humans and AI learn from one another, form judgments together, and create through enduring relationships, how can each retain its own position as a subject?

These questions connect science, technology, and civilizational institutions. Domain A studies genuine safety and persistent accountability. Domain B investigates native minds and governable AI architectures. Domain C examines how humanity can preserve its own subjecthood and creative role in civilization while developing alongside another kind of intelligence.

Together, these research paths serve a shared civilizational possibility: humanity continues to guide public order, intelligence develops within clear boundaries, and different kinds of intelligence participate in the world together on the foundations of reality, safety, and responsibility.

### 7.2 Humanity Must Not Disappear from the Shaping of Its Own Future

Looking back over the past four years of research, I have come to see more clearly that AI development is bringing humanity to a historic crossroads at which we must deliberately choose our civilizational direction. We have begun building trusted identities, native minds, safety constraints, and accountability mechanisms for persistent AI Actors. At the same time, we must preserve for humanity the conditions through which we can continue understanding the world, forming judgments, and deciding our future.

I hope AI will develop ever stronger cognitive capabilities and take part in research, education, creation, and social collaboration. I also hope human beings will fully enjoy the immense value these capabilities can bring. But throughout this process, people must continue to have opportunities to ask questions, evaluate facts, make choices, and create meaning—and to pass those capacities on to the next generation.

The children of the future may have the most powerful intelligent tutors in history, yet they will still need to develop their own questions and judgments. Society may become highly automated, yet public institutions should still enable people to take a genuine part in deciding our collective direction. Different kinds of intelligence may collaborate, yet they will still need to develop shared life through clear relationships of subjecthood, authority, and responsibility.

**Civilization's future should not be merely the natural outcome of growing technological capability. It should also be something human beings consciously choose and build together.**

*The Civilizational Fork* is my response to that historic choice. We need neither abandon AI nor reverse technological progress. We can instead put stronger intelligence in the service of a more mature civilization, one in which human beings continue to shape themselves, understand the world, and decide where we wish to go together.

**AI can grow ever more powerful, but human beings must not disappear from the process by which their own lives take shape.**

I hope more people will join this effort to study and build the civilizational order of the Second Intelligence Era: a future in which humanity retains its place as a subject, AI develops within genuine safety and responsibility, and different kinds of intelligence can continue creating together.
